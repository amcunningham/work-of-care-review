# The Work of Care: review protocol and first-pass findings

Nov 20, 2026 · Anne Marie Cunningham

## Short answer

Partly. A first-pass search found no single synthesis that asks whether organising care around eligibility, triage and risk selection reduces the capacity available to meet need. It found ten separate research traditions that each answer part of the question, several with trial-level evidence.

Five points stand out from the first pass and the full-text checks.

- The components of the argument are well evidenced separately. Adding a triage stage to general practice redistributes work and does not reduce it; case finding followed by assessment and case management does not reduce hospital use or cost; assessment programmes that are detached from delivery produce little benefit.
- The closest existing statement of the whole argument is the failure demand literature associated with John Seddon. It is largely consultancy case material, and the one academic test in UK primary care found the concept useful but hard to measure.
- Time-use studies of community nursing and care management exist, and one UK diary study compared two service models, but none found so far links the share of professional time spent on selection and assessment to outcomes across integrated and staged services. That comparison is the gap. The nearest direct tests come from US addiction services in the 1990s, where intake centralised and separate from treatment did not improve outcomes and, in one evaluation, was followed by lower attendance at treatment.
- A resource condition applies to both arrangements. Triage was adopted in general practice under workload pressure, and the model that removes it assumes that capacity matches demand. The integrated-team evidence has the same limit: the efficiency figures come from the Netherlands, UK pilots show acceptability only, and one east London team was limited by a caseload larger than planned.
- The one large direct comparison found does not show better outcomes from continuity. In the COFI study of 7,302 psychiatric inpatients in five countries ([Giacco et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1017/S2045796018000732)), rehospitalisation within a year did not differ between patients who had the same psychiatrist in hospital and in the community and those who had different ones; patients were more satisfied with continuity. The study covers one form of continuity in one specialty and did not report cost. The claim that integrated arrangements give better outcomes for the same resources remains a hypothesis.

The protocol below is designed to confirm or overturn that reading with database searches. Sections 1 to 5 are the protocol; the remaining sections report what the first pass found.

## Protocol 1: review question and scope

**Primary question.** How does the way a service divides the work of recognising need (selection, triage, assessment, eligibility decisions, risk stratification, care planning) from the work of responding to it affect patient outcomes, equity, patient and staff time, and total resource use?

**Sub-questions**

1. How much professional and patient time do selection and assessment processes consume, and how has this been measured?
2. When assessment is organisationally separate from treatment, how often does effective care follow?
3. What comparative evidence exists between integrated arrangements and staged arrangements?
4. Who is disadvantaged when access depends on passing a gateway?
5. Which theories explain when separating selection from delivery adds value and when it does not?

**Working definitions**

| Term | Definition for this review |
| --- | --- |
| Integrated arrangement | The professional or team that assesses also decides, treats and holds continuing responsibility (for example list-based general practice, district nursing, Buurtzorg teams) |
| Staged arrangement | Selection, assessment and delivery are done by different people, teams or organisations, with a decision point between each (for example triage hubs, referral management centres, single points of access, risk-stratified case finding followed by MDT review) |
| Gateway | Any classification, score, threshold or diagnosis that must be met before support is offered |
| Assessment-only contact | A contact whose output is a decision, plan or onward referral, with no treatment delivered |

**Scope.** Health care and adjacent human services (adult social care, mental health, welfare administration) in high-income countries; any study design; 1970 onwards, plus earlier foundational theory. Evidence on the accuracy of triage instruments and the performance of prediction models is excluded; the latter belongs to the first article.

**Two distinctions applied throughout**

- **Triage for safety and triage for rationing.** Safety triage identifies who cannot wait. Rationing triage decides who can be deflected, delayed or redirected because capacity is short. The two are extracted and synthesised separately; most emergency department evidence concerns the first and most general practice evidence the second.
- **Design and adequacy.** The review asks how a given capacity is divided between selecting and treating. Whether that capacity is adequate for the population's need is a separate question, recorded for every study so that the two are not confused.

## Protocol 2: review type and rationale

The proposed design is a critical interpretive synthesis with an initial meta-narrative mapping stage.

- **Why critical interpretive synthesis.** The question crosses disciplines that use different vocabularies and standards of evidence, and the aim is a synthesising argument with testable propositions, which a pooled effect size cannot provide. The method was developed for a similar problem; [Dixon-Woods et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-6-35) used it on access to healthcare and produced the concept of candidacy.
- **Why a meta-narrative mapping stage.** Each research tradition has framed the problem differently (rationing, waste, burden, coordination cost, targeting). Mapping the traditions first, following [Greenhalgh et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2004.12.001), prevents one framing from setting the terms for the rest.
- **Nested systematic review (optional).** Sub-question 3 could support a conventional systematic review if enough controlled comparisons of integrated and staged arrangements exist. The first pass suggests they are few and confined to particular settings.
- **Reporting and registration.** Report the mapping stage against the [RAMESES standards for meta-narrative reviews](https://doi.org/10.1186/1741-7015-11-20); register the protocol on OSF before formal searching.

A scoping review alone would catalogue the literatures without testing the argument. A realist review is a reasonable alternative if the preferred output is a set of context-mechanism-outcome statements about when separating selection from delivery works.

## Protocol 4: search, selection, extraction and appraisal

**Sources.** MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, HMIC, ASSIA, Social Policy and Practice, EconLit; forward and backward citation tracking from the seed sources in Scopus or Web of Science; grey literature from the NIHR Journals Library, National Audit Office, King's Fund, Nuffield Trust, Health Foundation, SCIE and Vanguard/Locality.

**Search concepts** (combined within each tradition, then across)

| Concept | Example terms |
| --- | --- |
| Selection and gateway processes | triage, total triage, single point of access, referral management, eligibility, threshold, gatekeeping, screening, risk stratification, case finding, candidacy |
| Assessment and planning | needs assessment, comprehensive assessment, care planning, multidisciplinary team meeting, case management |
| Organisation of work | division of labour, task separation, self-managing team, continuity, generalist, relational coordination, street-level bureaucracy |
| Costs of selection | administrative burden, transaction cost, coordination cost, failure demand, time and motion, workload, rejected referral, referral closed |
| Outcomes | outcomes, equity, utilisation, cost, patient time, staff time, value |

**Selection.** Sampling is purposive and iterative, as critical interpretive synthesis requires; papers are included for their contribution to the argument, with relevance judged first and quality second. Include any study or theoretical paper that describes, measures or compares the resource use or consequences of separating selection or assessment from delivery. Two reviewers screen a 20% sample independently; disagreements refine the criteria.

**Extraction.** Setting and service; arrangement (integrated, staged, hybrid); what the selection or assessment stage consists of and who does it; time or cost attributed to it; proportion of assessed people who go on to receive treatment; outcomes; equity findings; the authors' explanation of the result; study design.

Three further fields are extracted for every study: the stated reason the selection stage was introduced (safety, workload pressure, cost control, other); any measure of resource relative to need (funding or staff per weighted head, deprivation, baseline demand and waiting); and whether the purpose of triage was safety or rationing.

**Appraisal.** Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool for empirical studies; AMSTAR 2 for reviews; grey literature is appraised for source, method transparency and commercial interest. No study is excluded on quality alone, but weak evidence is labelled as such in the synthesis.

## Protocol 5: synthesis framework

Every finding is classified on two dimensions.

**Strength of claim**

1. *Established*: supported by trials, systematic reviews or consistent observational evidence.
2. *Inferred*: follows from established findings plus an explicit theoretical step, which is stated.
3. *Hypothesis*: plausible, with no direct comparative evidence found.

**Value domain affected**

| Domain | Question asked of each arrangement |
| --- | --- |
| Personal | Does the person get care that fits what matters to them, at an acceptable cost in their own time and effort? |
| Technical | For the people treated, are outcomes achieved with the least professional time? |
| Allocative | Is capacity distributed between selection, assessment, coordination and treatment, and between groups of patients, in the way that produces most benefit? |
| Societal | Are the costs of gateways distributed fairly, and what is the effect on carers, employers and other services? |

The synthesising argument is then tested against disconfirming cases: settings where a separate selection stage improves outcomes or safety.

## First-pass findings by literature

**Triage and demand management in general practice.** In the ESTEEM cluster trial across 42 practices, GP-led and nurse-led telephone triage of same-day requests increased primary care contacts over the following 28 days by 33% and 48% respectively (the count includes the triage call), at similar cost to usual care, about £75 per patient ([Campbell et al. 2014](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(14)61058-8>)). In 147 practices adopting a telephone-first system, face-to-face consultations fell by 38% and telephone consultations rose twelve-fold; mean GP time consulting rose by 8% (95% CI −1% to 17%), with no evidence of savings or reduced secondary care use ([Newbould et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j4197)). A modelling study drawing on a systematic review concluded that digital-first access is likely to increase GP workload unless contacts are shorter and more often resolved without a second consultation than published studies show ([Salisbury et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.2196/18203)).

**Referral management.** Referral management centres were often described as successful by those running them despite limited evidence of reduced referrals or savings ([Ball et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4806456)); a [Strategy Unit rapid review (2016)](https://strategyunitwm.nhs.uk/sites/default/files/2017-09/Referral-management-Oct16.pdf) reported that earlier reviews found no reduction in referrals from such centres.

**Removing triage.** Advanced access was built on the opposite premise. Its originators described staff time spent on triage and backlog management as resources diverted from patient care ([Murray and Tantau 2000](https://hqontario.ca/Portals/0/documents/qi/elearning-and-events/same-day-appointment-murray-tanta-en.pdf); [Murray and Berwick 2003](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.289.8.1035)). A systematic review found shorter waits and fewer missed appointments, with mixed or missing data on clinical outcomes ([Rose et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinternmed.2011.168)).

**Emergency departments: a disconfirming case.** Placing a senior doctor at triage reduced waiting time and length of stay across 25 mostly low-quality studies ([Abdulwahid et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/94877)). Here the front-door stage is done by someone who can start investigation and treatment, so selection and response are combined at first contact.

**Case finding and case management.** A meta-analysis of 36 studies found no significant effect of case management for at-risk patients on total cost, mortality or use of primary or secondary care, with small gains in satisfaction ([Stokes et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0132340)). A quasi-experimental evaluation of multidisciplinary team case management for high-risk patients found it did not meet its primary aim ([Stokes et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2015-010468)).

**Assessment with and without delivery.** In 106 UK practices and over 43,000 people aged 75 and over, universal versus targeted assessment, and management by a geriatric team versus the primary care team, produced almost no difference in outcomes ([Fletcher et al. 2004](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(04)17353-4>)). General health checks have little or no effect on mortality; the review authors suggest one reason is that primary care clinicians already identify and act on risk when they see patients for other reasons ([Krogsbøll et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009009.pub3)). Comprehensive geriatric assessment is effective where evaluation is linked to strong long-term management ([Stuck et al. 1993](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0140-6736(93)92884-v>)); the Cochrane review confirms benefit for inpatient assessment but was underpowered to separate wards that control delivery from teams that only advise ([Ellis et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006211.pub3)). Personalised care planning gave small improvements and worked best when integrated into routine care ([Coulter et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010523.pub2)).

**Integrated nursing and social care models.** Buurtzorg nurses do their own assessments as part of responsibility for the full range of care. In the KPMG analysis commissioned by the Dutch health ministry, Buurtzorg clients received about 108 hours of home care a year against about 168 for the average provider, and case-mix adjusted home-care cost was at the 38th percentile; once nursing home, physician and hospital costs were added, total cost per client was close to the national average (49th percentile). Overhead was reported at about 8% of costs against about 25% for other providers ([Gray, Sarnak and Burgers 2015](https://edagsorden.regionh.dk/committee_1444/agenda_254707/documents/6aab94a5-e4ce-4b25-a7b6-d7b95962819e.pdf)). Evidence from outside the Netherlands is 25 mostly descriptive publications ([Hegedüs et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijnsa.2022.100061)) and, in England, a single-team pilot ([Drennan et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-021931)). In adult social care, the Three Conversations approach starts from abandoning assessment for services; an NIHR-funded study produced a theory of change and noted little evidence so far on effectiveness ([Stevens et al.](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/122664)). The Choice and Partnership Approach in child mental health has 36 evaluations, with clinical outcomes too poorly measured for meta-analysis ([Campbell et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-064436)).

**Continuity.** Across more than 10 million consultations in 381 English practices, patients who saw their regular doctor went 18.1% longer before their next visit ([Kajaria-Montag et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2021.02015)).

**How much assessment leads to treatment.** In 2022–23, 39% of children referred to mental health services in England had the referral closed before treatment and 32% entered treatment ([Children's Commissioner 2024](https://www.childrenscommissioner.gov.uk/?p=34764)); closure before treatment has several causes, so this is an upper bound on assessment without response. For NHS Continuing Healthcare, [NHS Clinical Commissioners (2018)](https://basw.co.uk/sites/default/files/resources/basw_64703-7.pdf), citing the [NAO (2017)](https://www.nao.org.uk/reports/nhs-continuing-healthcare-investigation/), reported that 18% of those assessed were found eligible. The [Fonagy review (2026)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/independent-review-into-mental-health-conditions-adhd-and-autism-final-report) describes diagnosis as the primary gateway to support; its amended executive summary says diagnosis is perceived as a requirement for help and attributes rising demand to rising need and greater recognition (see the check of inferred rows below).

**Costs to applicants and unequal access.** Candidacy describes eligibility as negotiated between people and services ([Dixon-Woods et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-6-35)). Ethnography of admissions conferences showed social and moral judgements being used to rule patients in or out ([Hughes and Griffiths 1997](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0277-9536(96)00207-9>)). The administrative burden framework separates learning, compliance and psychological costs and has been applied to health care ([Herd and Moynihan 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6773.13858)).

**Targeting in social policy.** The administrative and transaction costs of targeting are a long-standing theme ([Mkandawire 2005](https://socialprotection.gov.bd/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Targeting-and-Universalism.pdf)). The claim that targeting reduces overall redistribution remains contested ([Gugushvili and Laenen 2021](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/CE85800A38AC83F9EEAA1AD45880CC47/core-reader)).

**Failure demand.** [Locality and Seddon (2014)](https://ccednet-rcdec.ca/resource/saving-money-by-doing-the-right-thing-why-local-by-default-must-replace-diseconomies-of-scale/) argue that repeat assessments and multiple referrals generate much of the demand on public services; the evidence is consultancy case material. An academic evaluation in UK primary care found the concept valuable and identified practical barriers to recording it ([Walley et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1108/IJHCQA-08-2017-0159)).

**A terminology problem.** "Integrated care" in the evaluation literature usually means coordination between providers, which often adds assessment and planning stages. A [2020 meta-analysis of 34 economic evaluations](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10198-020-01217-5) found modestly lower costs and better outcomes with high heterogeneity. Those results do not test the integrated arrangement defined in this protocol.

### The resource condition

Scarcity sits on both sides of the argument, and the synthesis has to keep the two apart.

- **Scarcity as the cause of triage.** The trial literature introduces telephone triage as something practices adopted to manage workload ([Campbell et al. 2014](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(14)61058-8>)) and telephone-first as a response to rising demand and recruitment difficulty ([Newbould et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j4197)). This matches Lipsky's account of rationing and screening as routines that front-line staff adopt when resources are inadequate. Level: inferred.
- **Adequacy as a condition of the alternative.** Advanced access is not sustainable where demand permanently exceeds capacity ([Murray and Berwick 2003](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.289.8.1035)). The model that removes triage therefore assumes the shortage is absent. Level: established as a stated design condition.
- **The limit of failure demand.** Removing failure demand frees capacity only up to the amount of failure demand present. Where need already exceeds capacity, redesign narrows the gap and cannot close it; and some failure demand (rejected referrals, repeat contacts after delay) is produced by the shortage itself. Level: inferred.
- **Removing a gateway moves the cost.** An unpublished 2015 board report on a systems-thinking learning disability pilot in Gwent records that dropping exclusion criteria to reduce hand-offs brought in a caseload of which about a third had a learning disability, with a noted impact on limited resources and an equalities risk for the original client group. It reported no outcome data or comparator. Level: single descriptive case.
- **The condition applies to integrated teams as well.** The efficiency figures for self-managing nursing teams come from Dutch data. A scoping review of 25 publications on Buurtzorg-derived models outside the Netherlands found satisfaction consistently reported and no reliable findings on cost-effectiveness or patient outcomes ([Hegedüs et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijnsa.2022.100061)). In one east London neighbourhood nursing team, a caseload larger than planned overburdened the team and limited the care nurses could give ([Lalani et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31818293/)). At another UK site efficiency could not be quantified or compared with usual district nursing ([Drennan et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-021931)). Level: inferred, from one qualitative study and the absence of UK efficiency data.

The combined proposition is that scarcity produces rationing triage, and rationing triage then uses some of the scarce capacity without reducing workload. Removing triage without adding capacity returns a service to the queue that triage replaced; the evidence found does not show that a queue is better.

The same condition holds for the integrated arrangement. An integrated team whose caseload exceeds its capacity is limited in the care it can give, and the evidence found does not show that integrated teams outperform staged services where capacity is short.

## Gaps and the comparative research question

**Gaps identified**

- Time-use studies classify work as direct care, indirect care, documentation and administration; none found so far separates selecting and assessing from treating, or relates the split to outcomes. The closest is a UK diary study of care managers, which found a clinical model in community mental health and an almost exclusively administrative one in older people's services, and called for comparison of their effectiveness.
- No routine measure of assessment-to-treatment conversion outside child mental health and Continuing Healthcare.
- Evaluations of triage count contacts and costs within one service; few follow the patient's whole episode or count patient time.
- Evidence on integrated models (Buurtzorg, Three Conversations, Choice and Partnership) is mostly descriptive, uncontrolled or from single sites.
- Equity effects of gateways are documented qualitatively; quantitative comparison between arrangements is rare.

**Research question.** For a defined population and type of need, do services in which the same professional or team assesses, decides, treats and keeps responsibility achieve better outcomes per unit of total resource than services that separate selection, assessment and delivery?

**Feasible designs**

1. Time-use study: structured activity sampling in matched district nursing, community mental health and general practice teams, classifying time by function.
2. Natural experiments: practices and areas adopting total triage, single points of access or neighbourhood hubs at different dates, analysed with difference-in-differences on episode-level contacts, time to definitive care, repeat contacts and outcomes, stratified by deprivation.
3. Conversion and re-contact indicators from routine data: proportion of referrals assessed and not treated; contacts per resolved episode; re-referral within 90 days.
4. A tracer condition followed end to end under each arrangement, counting professional and patient time.

**Confounding by indication.** Services under the most pressure are the ones that adopt triage, so they will compare badly on access and workload for reasons that precede it. Every comparative design above needs baseline measures of demand, capacity and waiting, adjustment or matching on them, and results stratified by resource relative to need. Without this, an association between staged arrangements and worse outcomes cannot be attributed to the arrangement.

**Measures to define in advance.** Selection share of workforce time; contacts and elapsed time to first treatment; proportion of assessments that change management; patient-reported outcome and burden; cost per resolved episode; gradients by deprivation.

## Limitations of this first pass and next steps

- The first pass used web search across 37 queries. A first PubMed run has since been added (see the search log); Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, HMIC, ASSIA and EconLit have not been searched, so the statement that no synthesis exists remains provisional.
- Seed sources were chosen from prior knowledge of each tradition and then checked; this favours well-known papers and UK primary care.
- Most sources were checked against abstracts, repository records or official summaries. Full text was read for the Commonwealth Fund case study only.

**What the abstracts show**

*Delay between first contact and treatment loses people (trial evidence)*

- [Maddux et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.3109/00952999509002714): 186 opioid users randomised to admission to methadone treatment in 1 day or 14 days. Dropout before medication was 4% against 26%; retention at one year did not differ significantly (43% against 39%).
- [Stasiewicz and Stalker 1999](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0306-4603(98)00082-3>): 128 people randomised; an intake appointment within 48 hours gave a higher attendance rate than later appointments, and reminder calls or cards added nothing.
- [Festinger et al. 1995](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0306-4603(94)00052-z>): among 235 callers, days to appointment was the only variable associated with attending intake; client characteristics were not.

*How many assessed people reach treatment*

- [Arfken et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/02791072.2002.10399934): of 5,004 people assessed at Detroit's centralised intake unit, 50.3% entered treatment within 30 days; 45% of women against 53% of men, a difference that held after adjustment.
- [Claus and Kindleberger 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/02791072.2002.10399933): after centralised assessment, a shorter wait predicted attending the first treatment appointment; people with a psychiatric comorbidity were less likely to enter treatment. The authors conclude that programme factors play a substantial part in engagement.
- [Capoccia et al. 2007](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s1553-7250(07)33011-0>): at one hospital, 25% of scheduled clients attended assessment and 19% reached treatment; assessment on arrival and same-day admission cut time from first contact to first treatment session from 4.1 to 1.3 days and reduced non-attendance.
- [Edbrooke-Childs et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/13591045211002609): of 23,300 young people in specialist mental health services in England, 46% attended a single session; this was more likely for younger children, Black young people and those with more complexity factors.

*A policy reversal*

- [Berends 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugpo.2019.09.009): Victoria, Australia introduced centralised intake and assessment for drug treatment in 2014, when demand exceeded supply. An independent review documented disruption to client flow, and in 2016 assessment was returned to treatment services.

*Other*

- [Raine et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/014107680509800404): GPs in England described a single point of access to mental health care as a barrier to professional relationships and to their accountability to patients.
- [Melathopolous and Cawthorpe 2019](https://doi.org/10.7812/TPP/18.215): after central intake for child mental health in a Canadian region, per capita discharges rose 1.8-fold over 15 years while investment rose 2.8-fold.
- [Madden et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.14198): an open-access methadone programme in Connecticut cut the average wait from 21 days to 0.3 while the patient census rose 183%.
- [Kvestad et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13063-024-08028-6): protocol for a Norwegian trial (n = 588) of an early assessment team that tries to resolve the presenting problem at first contact, against waiting list then diagnostic assessment then treatment. Results are not yet published; this is the closest trial to the comparison proposed here.

**Effect on the argument**

- A further mechanism is supported at the established level: each added stage adds delay and a handover, and delay before treatment causes attrition. This holds in randomised trials in addiction services; applying it to other services is an inference.
- Attrition is unequal. Women and people with psychiatric comorbidity were less likely to get from assessment to treatment.
- Attrition also changes the capacity argument. A staged system can appear to cope because people drop out between stages. [Rohrer et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1176/ps.47.11.1233) made this point: lower use after central intake may reduce cost but is not the intended outcome. Any comparison of arrangements therefore has to count people who were assessed and never treated.
- The addiction literature on reducing time to treatment (the NIATx programme) is a second source, with advanced access, on what happens when the selection stage is shortened or merged with treatment.

**A counter-argument found in the full texts**

[Salisbury et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.2196/18203) state that higher workload after an access change is not in itself an argument against it, because the extra consultations may be a response to unmet need; they argue such changes should be justified by benefit to patients and not by claims of reduced workload. The same applies to triage: more contacts after triage cannot be counted as waste unless outcomes or patient time are also measured. None of the three general practice studies read in full measured health outcomes beyond 28 days, and Newbould et al. measured none.

**Supply-related demand**

Both [Newbould et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j4197) and [Rocks et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-020-05463-4) describe easier first contact increasing the number of requests. This is a third route, with redistribution and attrition, by which a front-door stage changes the work a service does, and the review should extract it separately.

**Hughes and Griffiths 1997 in more detail**

The paper compares two selection settings and attributes the difference between them to how the work is organised. This is the comparison of arrangements that the review is looking for, at the scale of two case studies from 1989 to 1990.

- **Selector separate from advocate.** In cardiac catheterisation conferences, cardiologists presented patients they had already pre-selected to a single surgeon working under a capped number of operations. Social and moral information about patients surfaced almost only when the surgeon was seeking to rule a case out.
- **Selector also provides the care.** In neuro-rehabilitation admissions conferences, the consultant who presented cases also controlled the service patients would receive. Moral information was used mostly to rule patients in, to build a case for admitting the candidates staff favoured.
- **Queue and pool.** Cardiac surgery ran a queue; rehabilitation drew from a pool of candidates reviewed over several meetings. In the pool, flat rejection was rare; decisions were deferred, and deferral led to attrition as patients went home or were placed elsewhere. Of 50 successive referrals, 37 were formally accepted and 33 admitted.
- **Ruling in also excludes.** The authors point out that doing more for one patient disadvantages others, and that ruling in makes value-based selection less visible. On the cardiac waiting list mean age was 48.3 in the urgent category, 59.7 in routine and 61.5 on hold.
- **Selection shapes referral.** Decisions by the tertiary provider communicate acceptance criteria to referrers, who then pre-select. Most exclusion happened before the conference, through selective referral.
- **Scarcity is the stated context.** The authors describe a gap between need and available treatment in both services, managed through selective referral, rejection and waiting.

**Consequences for the argument**

- Integrated arrangements are not free of rationing. Where the selector also provides the care, selection takes the form of advocacy for favoured candidates and attrition of the rest, which is harder to see and to audit. The review should treat visibility of exclusion as an outcome.
- The paper supports attrition through deferral as a rationing mechanism in a UK service, alongside the trial evidence from addiction services.
- Fletcher 2004 reports a further handover loss: 30% of patients referred to the primary care team and 50% of those referred to the hospital geriatric team did not respond to the referral; the reason was unknown for most.

**Stuck et al. 1993, read in full**

The claim holds, with a limit on how far it can be pushed.

- **What was compared.** 28 controlled trials (4,959 intervention and 4,912 control subjects) in five programme types: inpatient geriatric units, inpatient consultation services, home assessment, post-discharge home assessment and outpatient assessment.
- **Units that assess and treat did better than services that assess and advise.** For inpatient units, the odds of living at home were 1.80 (95% CI 1.28 to 2.53) at 6 months and 1.68 (1.17 to 2.41) at 12 months, and mortality at 6 months was lower (odds ratio 0.65, 0.46 to 0.91). For inpatient consultation services, living at home at 6 months was 0.97 (0.75 to 1.25) and at 12 months 1.26 (0.86 to 1.85).
- **The covariate analysis.** Medical control over implementing recommendations was associated with better mortality and living location in several of the pooled analyses; ambulatory follow-up was associated with better mortality and physical function in some. The authors conclude that programmes with control over recommendations and extended follow-up were more likely to be effective.
- **The limit.** Control over recommendations was present in all six inpatient unit trials and in one of eight consultation service trials, so it cannot be separated from programme type. The authors used it as a programme-level proxy because most investigators had not recorded whether individual recommendations were implemented. Heterogeneity tests were positive for several pooled estimates.

Taken with Ellis 2017, the position is: the 1993 analysis found the pattern, at programme level and confounded with setting; the 2017 update, with individual patient data from five trials, could not confirm a ward versus team difference because team trials were too few. The statement in the three-level table should read "geriatric assessment was more effective in programmes that also controlled treatment and follow-up; this has not been confirmed in later analyses", at the boundary between established and inferred.

**Referral management: the underlying reviews**

- [Blank et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3310/hsdr03240) (scientific summary and the referral management centre passages read; the full report runs to several hundred pages): a systematic review of 290 studies of interventions to manage referral. Six studies concerned referral management centres or other major triage systems. The authors rate the evidence as very mixed: the studies were divided on whether there was a positive effect on referral outcomes, and two of those showing a positive effect were at higher risk of bias. In the largest evaluation they describe, covering 85 practices in five groups, outpatient attendance rates rose in four groups, and after correction one group, a referral management centre, still showed a significant increase (1.05 attendances per 1,000 persons per month, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.64).
- [Winpenny et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3310/hsdr04150): only the plain English summary could be retrieved. It states that there is limited information on whether new schemes increase or reduce demand or cost. Its referral management substudy is the interview study published as Ball et al. 2016.

The row in the three-level table now reads: evidence that referral management centres reduce referrals is very mixed, and one large evaluation found increased outpatient attendance. Level: established that benefit is unproven; not established that they cause harm or waste.

**Krogsbøll et al. 2019** (113 pages; abstract, summary of results, discussion and conclusions read)

- Confirmed. 17 trials, 15 with outcome data, 251,891 participants. Total mortality risk ratio 1.00 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.03; high certainty); no effect on cancer mortality, and probably none on cardiovascular mortality, ischaemic heart disease or stroke.

- Follow-up tests and referrals after screening were rarely reported, so the trials cannot show what the checks cost the health service. The authors treat more diagnosis and treatment without health gain as overdiagnosis.

**Kajaria-Montag et al. 2024** (accepted manuscript dated May 2023; abstract, methods outline, counterfactual analysis and implications read)

- Confirmed. Among patients with at least three consultations in two years, the interval to the next visit was 18.1% longer (95% CI 16.9% to 19.2%) after seeing the regular doctor, with consultation length marginally shorter. The effect was larger for older patients, those with several chronic conditions and those with mental health conditions.
- The unit of analysis is the consultation. The authors describe the ideal experiment as random assignment of a single consultation to the regular doctor or another doctor, and say the study does not address the long-term effect of receiving more or less continuity.
- Acuity is unmeasured. The authors address this with higher-acuity subsamples and two instrumental-variable models; the design remains observational.
- The 5.2% reduction in consultation demand is stated as an upper bound. It assumes continuity can be raised to the level of the top tenth of practices and directed at the patients who benefit most, with no adverse effect on other aspects of practice productivity; the authors note that trade-offs at practice level may exist.
- The authors contrast two ways of organising practice under shortage: maximising consultations per physician day with fast access to any doctor, and organising around access to the patient's own doctor. They argue that the first may generate avoidable later demand. This is the nearest statement in the operations literature to the argument of this review, from data on general practice in England.

**Independent review into mental health conditions, ADHD and autism (Fonagy, 2026), full report.**

- Figures confirmed in the full text: more than 254,000 people with an open autism referral by December 2025, over 90% waiting longer than 13 weeks; 40% of clinicians surveyed reported no ongoing post-diagnostic support and 12.6% described support extending beyond a single session; about 55% reported routine waiting-list support; independent providers carry out more than half of NHS-funded ADHD assessments and more than a third of autism assessments; spending on independent-sector ADHD provision rose by about 252% in three years, with a projected overspend of £164 million across the integrated care boards analysed.
- The clinician figures come from a survey of clinicians, so they describe reported practice, not measured provision.
- On diagnosis as a gateway, the full report goes further than the executive summary. The summary says diagnosis "has increasingly been perceived as a requirement" for help through education, health and care plans, Access to Work and parts of the welfare system. Chapter 13 states that once documentation becomes the principal route to support, demand for diagnosis increases, and that families, schools and clinicians respond rationally to these arrangements. The introduction presents the same claim as one of three explanations for rising demand and says that none of the three, alone, explains the evidence.
- The basis for the Chapter 13 statement is indirect: studies showing that statutory documentation changes the support pupils receive, the Public Accounts Committee's conclusion that parents seek plans because SEND Support cannot be relied on, and testimony. The report does not present a study that estimates how much of the rise in assessment demand is attributable to gateway rules.
- A clinician roundtable quotation describes services that previously employed staff for post-diagnostic support and now say the waiting list is so large that they can only do the diagnosis. This is the displacement mechanism of interest (assessment work replacing support work under pressure), reported as testimony.
- The report's position on the alternative is conditional. It states that a needs-led approach without timely diagnosis risks becoming generic care or implicit rationing, and that a diagnosis-led approach without support produces long waits and labels without help. It recommends a "needs-led plus diagnosis" system in which support starts when need is identified, with specialist capacity protected for people with the most severe needs.
- It also states that expanding assessment capacity through Right to Choose increased costs without necessarily meeting individual needs better.

Effect on the table: the row on gateway classification increasing demand moves from hypothesis to inferred, with the source described as an independent review's stated conclusion resting on indirect evidence and testimony. The displacement of post-diagnostic support by assessment remains at the level of clinician report. The review's warning about implicit rationing under a needs-led model is added to the resource condition.

**[Walley et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1108/IJHCQA-08-2017-0159), failure demand in primary care.**

- Design: case study and commentary; an expert panel of about 90 on the 2014/15 winter pressures, 15 patient interviews, and national NHS 111 and emergency department data.
- Findings: primary care records activity, not demand, so failure demand cannot be measured from routine data; failure demand is attributed partly to existing demand and capacity management practices, and is deflected to NHS 111 and emergency departments, particularly when primary care capacity is reduced; 45 to 55% of NHS 111 calls ended in a recommendation to attend primary care; advanced access as implemented in England, with embargoed same-day appointments, is described as converting routine demand into urgent demand.

- The authors argue that failure demand should be used as a diagnostic measure and not as a target, and note that the national indicator on avoidable contact (NI14) was introduced in 2008 and dropped in 2010.

Effect on the table: the failure demand row stays at inferred. **[Bolton et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0021886321991597), systematic review of relational coordination.**

### Fifth batch: open-access papers from the included set

Sixteen further included records were read from full text (methods, results and discussion; long results sections were read in part). They were chosen as open-access substitutes for the addiction intake papers that could not be obtained, and to test the child mental health, general practice and integrated team rows.

**Central intake and single-entry models**

- [Isaacs et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37174264/): scoping review, 34 sources, 14 of them evaluations. Reported benefits are mostly access measures (shorter waits, simpler entry). Reported problems match the mechanisms in this review: where intake and service delivery were run by different organisations, access fell because clients did not travel on to the second site; high demand left insufficient treatment slots after intake; one model was withdrawn. The authors state that waiting time for already overloaded mental health services is not affected by central intake, that central intake can increase demand, and that it may improve access without changing outcomes. No formal quality appraisal.
- [Damani et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31874866/): before-and-after study of a central intake service for joint replacement in Winnipeg (2,397 patients before, 2,282 after). Variation in waiting time between surgeons fell; waits for knee replacement fell by 18 to 22%; throughput did not increase. The stated mechanism is pooling: referral to the next available surgeon rose to 40% of referrals.
- [Vo et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35034657/): cohort from a central intake clinic for hip and knee replacement (808 and 1,159 patients). Priority scoring ordered the wait for consultation (median 49, 75 and 194.5 days for hip priorities 1 to 3). No concurrent control; comparison is with historical regional figures.
- [Kvestad et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38468321/): this is a trial protocol, with no results. It is a randomised trial (target 588) in Norwegian adult mental health comparing an early assessment meeting within two to three weeks, by two therapists working with other agencies, against the usual waiting list. It is the closest design found to a direct test and should be followed for results.

Reading: pooled entry with priority scoring reduces variation and orders the queue for a single, well-defined procedure. In mental health and addiction services the same structure improves entry and leaves the wait for treatment unchanged where treatment capacity is the constraint. This is consistent with the distinction between a queue and a pool in Hughes and Griffiths 1997, and it marks a boundary condition for the argument.

**Child and youth mental health**

- [Fazel et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33951078/): mixed-methods evaluation in Buckinghamshire and Oxfordshire with a difference-in-differences analysis. After a single point of access with self-referral, new patients rose 12% (and 19% by year three); waiting time to first contact rose 13% in year one and was 21% lower by year three; waiting time to second contact did not change; re-referral did not change. Spend per head of target population rose 7% and 22% over the period, and staff described work in the lower-intensity pathway as having increased sharply. The improvement in first contact cannot be separated from the funding increase, and the unchanged wait for second contact fits the pattern of faster entry without faster treatment.
- [Maxwell et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30236167/): historical control study of the Norfolk youth service (ages 14 to 25). Referrals rose from 7,476 to 12,559; the proportion accepted fell from 90.8% to 68.2% (from 78.5% to 59.2% for under-18s); contacts per referral fell for 17-year-olds from 11.3 to 8 and rose for 18-year-olds from 5.3 to 7. The authors say the reason for the rise in referrals is unclear and may reflect national trends. An open entry route was followed by more referrals, a lower acceptance rate and fewer contacts per accepted young person in the younger group.
- [Elkjaer et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41723401/): qualitative interviews with 10 hospital triage consultants in Denmark. Referrals are sorted first by secretarial staff into subspecialties and then assessed by specialists against subspecialty criteria. Consultants describe assessing referrals on two grounds, clinical information and departmental capacity, and describe subspecialty criteria as fitting patients with complex presentations poorly, with rejection used in place of dialogue with the GP.
- [Al-Asadi et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24938311/): 58.7% of 9,394 people who completed an online assessment did not start treatment. The setting is an online self-help programme, so relevance to staffed services is limited.

**General practice**

- [Murdoch et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25887747/): process evaluation within ESTEEM. Two organisational forms of GP triage are described: a duty doctor system and an integrated system in which GPs triaged their own patients alongside normal surgery. In the duty system, time spent triaging was time lost to surgery, which reduced face-to-face appointments and moved work to the other GPs. In practices with personal lists, GPs reported that triage reduced bookable appointments with the patient's own doctor to an extent they would not accept. Nurse triage needed extra nursing resource to be workable. This is direct qualitative evidence that a separate triage role consumes appointment capacity, and that the trial contained an integrated variant that was not analysed separately.
- [Smart et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36410768/): small qualitative study of online consultation. GPs reported more control over workload under total triage; administrative staff reported more work, much of it not visible, and having to ask clinical questions. Practices that ran online consultation as an additional route alongside existing access reported more overload than those using a single triaged route.
- [Bang et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33144371/): Danish cohort, 33 advanced access practices matched with 66 others, 449,738 patients. No significant change in daytime consultations (adjusted rate ratio 1.03, 0.99 to 1.07) or out-of-hours use.
- [Eccles et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38242712/): scoping review, 49 studies, 33 from the UK. The stated purpose of access systems was most often to manage demand and improve efficiency (28 studies); patient-centred aims were stated in 17. Only six studies examined use by specific patient groups.
- [Ansell et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427444/): systematic review, 11 studies, all of open access scheduling; mean reduction in wait of 11.3 days. All are uncontrolled before-and-after comparisons, and the review does not examine workload or continuity.

**Integrated self-managing teams**

- [Hegedüs et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38745628/): scoping review of 25 publications on Buurtzorg-derived models outside the Netherlands. Patient satisfaction is consistently reported. Effects on admissions, hours of care and productivity are described as not conclusive and varying between sites; one study found no cost change at one year; the authors state that reliable findings on cost-effectiveness and patient outcomes from outside the Netherlands are lacking.
- [Lalani et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31818293/): qualitative evaluation of one neighbourhood nursing team in east London. Staff and patients reported better experience and less duplication; a caseload larger than planned overburdened the team and limited the care nurses could give; nurses reported a greater administrative burden and did not feel able to exercise the autonomy managers intended.
- [Drennan et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29880574/): mixed-methods study at one site. The model was feasible and acceptable; effectiveness and efficiency could not be quantified, costed or compared with usual district nursing because routine data lacked casemix.

- [Adadja et al. 2026](https://www.annfammed.org/content/24/3/239.full.pdf): read in full from the PDF supplied (text and Tables 1 to 5; the supplementary risk-of-bias tables were not supplied). **Campbell et al. 2015, the full ESTEEM report** ([DOI 10.3310/hta19130](https://doi.org/10.3310/hta19130)). Read from the published PDF: the scientific summary, the introduction, the process evaluation sections on implementation, experience of triage and what influenced it, and from the discussion the summary of findings, the workload section, the process evaluation section and the opening of the implications for health care. Not read: methods, the results tables, the detail of the economic evaluation, safety and the appendices.

- **Contacts.** Mean primary care contacts over 28 days were 1.91 with usual care, 2.65 with GP triage and 2.81 with nurse triage (rate ratios 1.33 and 1.48). The authors describe this as additional, not substituted, workload. When all contacts in the practice on the first day were counted as one, the increase was 10% and 12%.
- **How far triage resolved the request.** 51% of usual care patients had one contact only in 28 days, against 23% with GP triage and 12% with nurse triage. Among patients managed per protocol, 21% under GP triage and 8% under nurse triage had no further contact after the triage call.
- **Clinician time.** Estimated contact time on the first day was 9.6 minutes with usual care, 10.3 with GP triage and 14.8 with nurse triage. GP time was 9.1, 9.0 and 7.7 minutes, so GP triage saved almost no GP time and nurse triage moved time to nurses (7.1 minutes). Face-to-face GP consultations after triage were longer than first consultations in usual care.
- **Cost.** About £75 per patient in each arm. The report adds a caveat not in the short summaries: GP consultations for same-day requests in usual care averaged 9.5 minutes against the 11.7 minutes assumed in the unit cost, and on the shorter figure usual care would be the least costly arm.
- **Other services and safety.** No difference in out-of-hours, walk-in or emergency department contacts, deaths or emergency admissions.
- **Process evaluation** (84 interviews; eight intervention practices among ten sampled). Two of the four nurse triage practices added no nursing capacity; nurses worked unpaid extra hours, chronic disease management was neglected, and one practice paused the trial so that nurses could catch up. Under duty-doctor triage, surgery appointments were lost while the doctor triaged; one practice cited 51 slots a week and employed locums. Appointments bookable in advance were reduced in most GP triage practices, which staff in practices with personal lists saw as a loss of continuity. Under nurse triage, doctors often took the history again. Staff in five of the eight practices said appointments had not fallen; one nurse triage practice reported that they had halved.
- **Conditions for success.** The authors list adequate staff capacity, and a prior assessment of needs and capacity, among the factors that affected how triage was experienced. They also state that no consistent pattern explained success: the practice that reported halved appointments had also added no nursing capacity.
- **Why triage was adopted.** The introduction presents triage as a response to rising workload after the 2004 contract. One GP voiced the concern that better access through triage would increase demand.

**Effect on the three-level table.** The established row on triage and workload is confirmed and now has contact time as well as contact counts. The report supports the resource condition in part: capacity is named as a factor, and the costs of unfunded triage (unpaid hours, displaced chronic disease work, lost appointments) are described; the authors do not claim that capacity determines whether triage works. The cost-neutral finding should be quoted with the consultation-length caveat.

Triage decisions are made on what the patient writes; patients who cannot produce a complete or persuasive account may be deprioritised or misdirected, and this is patterned by disadvantage. Staff who know the patient can compensate in some cases.
- [Liberati et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2022.114711): qualitative study of access to secondary mental health care during the pandemic. Services became less permeable and finding a way in took more work from users and carers; the authors relate this to resource deficits that predated the pandemic.
- [Lindsay et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-024-03456-2): realist review of 197 papers on repeated missed appointments in primary care, using candidacy.
- [Sinnott et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3399/BJGPO.2024.0184): catalogue of 449 sources on efforts to improve access to UK general practice, 1984 to 2023.

**S6, administrative burden on patients.**

- [Kyle et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/haschl/qxaf216): scoping review, 63 studies. Measures of administrative cost count clinicians and organisations and are likely to understate the total, because patients' work is not counted.
- [Ilea et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2024.116686): scoping review, 31 studies; 28 measured burden and three tested an intervention to reduce it.
- [Rockwell et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/fmch-2024-002933): in bowel cancer screening, reported administrative burden was associated with delayed or forgone screening (odds ratio 1.42, 95% CI 1.28 to 1.57).

**S8, street-level bureaucracy.**

- [Blackwell-Green et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03201-z): scoping review of primary care receptionists and migrant access. Receptionists used discretion more often to obstruct registration than to help it, shaped by practice priorities and wider constraints.
- [Worthing et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3399/BJGP.2022.0336): qualitative study in north-east London. Staff were reluctant to register people without documents, who were seen as a burden or as less deserving of finite resources; participants described high workload and insufficient funding.
- [Simpson 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2648.2005.03636.x): case study in one English trust. The duties of the care co-ordinator role, with heavy workloads, left community psychiatric nurses unable to deliver psychosocial interventions.
- [Belling et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1186/1748-5908-6-23) (found through the T7 search): in community mental health teams, staffing levels, caseloads and administrative duties were barriers to continuity.

**S14, relational coordination.** The 60 titles were mostly cross-sectional studies of teamwork and staff outcomes in hospitals. None measured the share of capacity spent on selection or compared arrangements. Not pursued.

**T7, split between assessment or admission and continuing treatment in mental health.** The revised terms found a direct comparison.

- [Giacco et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1017/S2045796018000732) (the COFI study): natural experiment in five European countries, 7,302 patients. Rehospitalisation within a year was 38.9% where the same psychiatrist treated the patient in hospital and outside it, and 37.1% where different psychiatrists did (adjusted figures; odds ratio 1.08, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.25). Inpatient days, involuntary readmission, adverse events and social situation did not differ. Read in full: 57 hospitals; 87% followed up; no significant difference in any country or subgroup, and the point estimates in England (1.23, 0.96 to 1.58) and among people on benefits (1.18, 0.98 to 1.43) were in the direction of more readmission under continuity. Limits stated by the authors: allocation was by locality only in England and Italy, and elsewhere could reflect clinical decision or patient choice; outcomes were measures of service use, with no symptom measure; the economic analysis was still in progress. The authors advise against spending resources on changing this aspect of organisation. They also cite an earlier systematic review (Omer et al.) in which each study compared a newly introduced arrangement with the existing one and outcomes favoured the new arrangement whichever it was; this applies equally to the pilot studies of integrated models relied on elsewhere in this document.
- [Bird et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.2017.20): 2,709 patients from the same study. Satisfaction with inpatient treatment was higher with the same psychiatrist across settings; the difference in length of stay was not significant after allowing for clustering by hospital.
- [Klingemann et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-019-01757-z): qualitative arm. Patients and clinicians reported better relationships and transitions with personal continuity, and more choice and specialist expertise with the split model.
- [Begum et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6963-13-362): survey in four health boards; 76% of patients preferred one consultant throughout.

**Effect on the three-level table.**

- The hypothesis that integrated arrangements give better outcomes per unit of resource now has a contrary result. The largest direct comparison found (COFI) showed no difference in outcomes at one year, with higher patient satisfaction under continuity. Its scope is continuity of psychiatrist between hospital and community; it did not measure cost or the time spent on assessment.
- The gateways and equity rows gain Dakin 2024, Liberati 2022 and the two studies of reception staff.
- The resource condition gains qualitative support from Worthing 2023, Liberati 2022, Simpson 2005 and Belling 2011.
- The gap on uncounted patient work is supported by Kyle 2025, from United States sources only.
